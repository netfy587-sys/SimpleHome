Notes on chess strategy

Sixty-four squares, and still no one has solved it

A page on the fundamentals — how the pieces move, how games get recorded, and the handful of opening principles that hold up no matter who's across the board.

16pieces per side

A game of ideas, not luck

Chess has no hidden information and no element of chance — both players see the same board, and every mistake is entirely one's own. That's part of why it has held attention for over a thousand years: the game rewards understanding, not fortune.

What makes it endlessly replayable is scale. The number of possible legal positions is larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe, which is why even engines that calculate millions of moves a second haven't "solved" it.

How each piece moves

Pawn

One square forward, captures diagonally

Knight

An L-shape — two squares, then one across

Bishop

Any distance, diagonally only

Rook

Any distance, horizontally or vertically

Queen

Any distance, any direction

King

One square, any direction

The opening

Four principles that rarely fail

  1. Control the centrePieces placed near the middle four squares influence more of the board than pieces on the edge.
  2. Develop before you attackGet knights and bishops into play before moving the same piece twice or chasing an early attack.
  3. Castle earlyMoving the king to safety and connecting the rooks usually matters more than grabbing material.
  4. Don't move the queen out too soonAn early queen sortie tends to get chased around the board, losing time.

A few well-known openings

1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5

Ruy López

One of the oldest recorded openings, pressuring the knight defending e5 from the flank.

1. e4 c5

Sicilian Defence

The most popular reply to e4 at top level — sharp, asymmetric, and fights for the centre from the side.

1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6

King's Indian Defence

Black concedes the centre early, then counterattacks it later with pawns and pieces.
